THE FIFTY-NINE DAY SHOUT

A Kiwi Pub Political Comedy
Fictional political satire. The election figures used in the play — 3.6 million enrolled, 2 million voting, 1 million supporting independents, and 71 independent electorate victories — are hypothetical dramatic assumptions, not presented here as verified election forecasts or results.

Characters

BILLY — The pub philosopher. Quick with a joke, quicker with arithmetic.

ROD — Arrives carrying 169 pages of Cabinet documents.

KARL — Independent candidate with one very large idea about Marsden Point.

MARGE — Publican. Understands democracy primarily through who gets the next round.

HARRY — Regular. Suspicious of any document containing the word “Appendix”.

THE ELECTORAL GHOST — Appears whenever someone says “regulated period”.

71 INDEPENDENTS — Eventually arrive. This causes parking problems.

SCENE ONE — THE PUB

Friday evening. MARGE is polishing a glass. BILLY sits at the bar. ROD enters carrying an enormous folder.

BILLY
What have you got there, mate?

ROD
Government papers.

BILLY
Good grief. How many?

ROD
One hundred and sixty-nine pages.

BILLY
Mate, if democracy needs 169 pages to explain what happened to it, democracy needs a drink.

MARGE
What are you having?

ROD
Something that lasts fifty-nine days.

MARGE
That's not a drink. That's a marriage.

SCENE TWO — THE FIFTY-NINE DAYS

BILLY
Right. Explain it slowly. Pretend I'm a voter.

ROD
You are a voter.

BILLY
Exactly. That's why you have to explain it slowly.

ROD
Under the old arrangement, a new political party could work toward registration until Writ Day. Then the law changed. The registration gate was moved earlier. For the 2026 election, the effect is about fifty-nine days.

BILLY
Fifty-nine days?

ROD
Yes.

BILLY
Where did they put them?

ROD
What?

BILLY
The fifty-nine days. They must be somewhere. You can't just lose fifty-nine days. I've lost a weekend before. Never fifty-nine days.

HARRY
I lost 1987.

MARGE
We know, Harry.

BILLY
So what's the official explanation?

ROD
Administrative efficiency.

The pub goes silent.

HARRY
That's serious.

BILLY
Very. Whenever somebody says “administrative efficiency”, check your wallet.

SCENE THREE — THE BLACKBOARD

BILLY gets up and walks to the pub blackboard.

3,600,000 ENROLLED2,000,000 VOTE1,000,000 CHOOSE INDEPENDENTS

BILLY
Now, this is our imaginary election tonight. Three-point-six million enrolled. Say two million actually turn up. And suppose — just suppose — one million people decide:

“I'm voting for the local independent.”

HARRY
One million?

BILLY
Hypothetical, Harry. This is theatre. We can afford one million voters. Hollywood has dragons. We've got Huntly.

SCENE FOUR — THE TWO TICKS

BILLY
Now, under MMP you've normally got two votes. One electorate vote. One party vote.

MARGE
Two ticks.

BILLY
Correct. But Rod here has brought us a protest proposition.

ROD
One tick for the independent electorate candidate. Leave the party vote blank.

MARGE
So that's deliberately not using the second vote?

ROD
As a symbolic protest against the established-party system.

BILLY
Now hold on. Important public-service announcement from the pub: nobody has to do that. A voter can use both votes. A voter can choose a party and an independent electorate candidate. Or make another lawful choice. This is a political protest idea being argued in this play, not instructions from the Electoral Commission.

HARRY
Very responsible.

BILLY
I have my moments. Usually between beers.

SCENE FIVE — THE GREAT INDEPENDENT EXPERIMENT

ROD
Imagine one million electorate votes spread strategically across independent candidates.

BILLY
How many electorates?

ROD
Seventy-one.

BILLY
Seventy-one independents win?

ROD
That's the hypothetical.

71 INDEPENDENT MPs

HARRY
No party whips?

BILLY
Nope.

HARRY
No caucus telling everyone how to vote?

BILLY
Well, they'd still have to organise themselves somehow. Otherwise Question Time would take until Easter.

SCENE SIX — ELECTION NIGHT

A television appears.

ANNOUNCER
Breaking news. In an unprecedented result, independent candidates have won seventy-one electorates.

MARGE
Seventy-one?

ANNOUNCER
Yes. Parliament's seating plan has been declared a national emergency.

The pub erupts.

BILLY
Look at that! Seventy-one independent MPs. Half of them don't even know where Bellamy's is yet.

HARRY
They will. Give them a week.

SCENE SEVEN — KARL ARRIVES

Doors burst open. KARL enters carrying rolled engineering drawings.

KARL
Evening.

BILLY
Who's this?

ROD
Karl.

BILLY
What's under your arm?

KARL
Marsden Point.

BILLY
You got the whole refinery under there?

KARL
Plans.

BILLY
Oh good. For a moment I thought parking was about to become difficult.

SCENE EIGHT — WHO FORMS THE GOVERNMENT?

MARGE
Right. We've got seventy-one independents. What happens now?

BILLY
That's when constitutional reality becomes more complicated than pub arithmetic. MPs would have to determine who commands the confidence of the House. The Governor-General doesn't simply crown whoever has the loudest mates at the bar.

HARRY
Spoilsport.

ROD
In our fictional result, the seventy-one independents meet. They choose Karl as their leader.

KARL
I didn't get a text about this.

BILLY
Congratulations, Prime Minister. Your first constitutional crisis is the group chat.

SCENE NINE — THE INDEPENDENTS' MEETING

Seventy-one chairs. Seventy-one independent MPs. No two agree on anything.

INDEPENDENT 1
I want Finance.

INDEPENDENT 2
I want Health.

INDEPENDENT 3
I want Fisheries.

INDEPENDENT 4
I want the chair near the window.

KARL
Everybody settle down. We are not forming a political party.

INDEPENDENT 17
Thank goodness. We might miss the registration deadline.

Huge laugh.

SCENE TEN — PRIME MINISTER KARL

KARL
If I have the confidence of the House, I will recommend Ministers and build a Cabinet capable of governing. Not because everybody agrees on everything. But because seventy-one communities sent seventy-one representatives here.

BILLY
There it is. The great experiment. Can seventy-one independent minds behave themselves long enough to pass a Budget?

HARRY
Can ordinary parties?

BILLY
Fair point.

SCENE ELEVEN — MARSDEN POINT

KARL unrolls an enormous plan.

KARL
First major project. Marsden Point.

MARGE
The refinery?

KARL
Our fictional government wants domestic refining capability restored and developed. We commission the engineering work. We determine what can be reused. We calculate capital costs. We examine crude supply. We examine environmental requirements. We examine fuel security. Then Parliament decides whether the project stacks up.

BILLY
Hang on. You're Prime Minister in a comedy. You're supposed to announce something impossible by Tuesday.

KARL
No. Engineering first.

BILLY
This government won't last.

SCENE TWELVE — THE GHOST APPEARS

Lights dim.

ELECTORAL GHOST
Fifty-nine daaaaaays...

HARRY
Who's that?

BILLY
The regulated period. Ignore him.

ROD
That's the whole point. If citizens believe the established parties made it harder for future competitors to enter the party-vote contest, they can challenge that politically and legally. But the response still has to be democratic.

Voting.
Organising.
Information.
Court proceedings where justified.
Public scrutiny.

BILLY
And comedy.

ROD
And comedy.

SCENE THIRTEEN — BILLY EXPLAINS THE PROTEST

BILLY walks to the front of the stage.

BILLY
So here's the proposition being argued tonight. Not a command. Not Electoral Commission advice. A proposition.

Suppose a voter says:

“I'm tired of the established political machinery.”

That voter might decide to support an independent electorate candidate. Someone else might vote for an independent candidate and still use their party vote. Someone else may support a registered party. That's democracy.

But Rod's protest idea is:

one deliberate electorate tick for the independent, while intentionally declining to express a party preference.

And the political message he's trying to send is:

“I turned up. I participated. But I chose not to endorse a party.”

MARGE
Wouldn't leaving the party vote blank mean it doesn't help determine the proportional allocation of party seats?

BILLY
Exactly. That's the trade-off. If you're proposing a protest strategy, you've got to tell people what the protest costs as well as what it says.

ROD
Fair.

SCENE FOURTEEN — ONE MILLION

1,000,000

BILLY
And now comes the impossible pub question. What if one million people did something similar? Not because somebody ordered them to. Because they independently decided that local representation mattered more to them.

MARGE
Would that automatically elect seventy-one independents?

BILLY
No. That's where the beer-mat mathematics ends. Every electorate is decided separately. An independent actually has to win more electorate votes than every other candidate in that seat.

HARRY
So one million votes badly distributed could win very little.

BILLY
Correct. One candidate could get 900,000 votes in Hamilton and still only win Hamilton.

HARRY
That's democracy being inefficient again.

BILLY
Geographically inefficient.

SCENE FIFTEEN — THE HYPOTHETICAL LANDSLIDE

ROD
But in our play, the votes distribute perfectly. Seventy-one communities. Seventy-one independent victories.

BILLY
Because otherwise Act Three is just everyone watching television and going home disappointed.

SCENE SIXTEEN — PARLIAMENT WITHOUT WHIPS

The debating chamber.

SPEAKER
Order!

71 INDEPENDENTS
Why?

SPEAKER
Because that's literally my job.

KARL
Members — we have no traditional governing-party caucus. So legislation must win support issue by issue.

INDEPENDENT 22
What if we disagree?

KARL
Vote against it.

INDEPENDENT 22
We're allowed?

KARL
You're Members of Parliament. That's rather the idea.

Everyone looks shocked.

SCENE SEVENTEEN — THE FIRST CABINET

KARL
I need Ministers. Who among you understands health?

Three hands.

KARL
Finance?

Five hands.

KARL
Justice?

ROD slowly raises his hand from the public gallery.

SPEAKER
You're not an MP.

ROD
Worth a try.

KARL
Energy and industrial development?

Everyone points at Karl.

KARL
Right. Marsden Point again.

SCENE EIGHTEEN — BACK AT THE PUB

MARGE
So what's the moral?

BILLY
There isn't one. It's New Zealand politics. We have inquiries instead.

ROD
The point is that representation ultimately belongs to voters. Electoral law provides the structure. Parties provide one way of organising representation. Independents provide another.

BILLY
And if citizens think the rules unfairly protect people already inside Parliament — ask questions. Get the Cabinet papers. Challenge decisions lawfully. Stand candidates. Vote. And understand exactly what each vote does before you use — or deliberately don't use — it.

FINALE

BILLY raises his glass.

BILLY
To fifty-nine days.

MARGE
Why are we drinking to them?

BILLY
We're not. We're looking for them.

CHORUS

Seventy-one seats and seventy-one dreams,
Politics isn't as tidy as Cabinet seems.

One million voters may thunder and shout,
But every electorate still must be fought out.

A tick has a purpose, a blank has one too,
So know what your ballot will actually do.

And if Karl ends up in the Prime Minister's chair—

KARL
First Cabinet meeting at Marsden Point!

BILLY
Mate... First build the Cabinet. Then build the refinery.

Blackout.

END

The Fifty-Nine Day Shout — fictional political satire for discussion of the 2026 New Zealand election.
